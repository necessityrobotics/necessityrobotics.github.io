Who we are

A robotics company.

Necessity Robotics is building robots for the physical world: humanoids, robots for the edge cases that are too dangerous or too hard for people, and surveillance technology for defence.

The hardest problem we are working on is the hand: picking up and handling real objects as well as a person does. The bar we hold ourselves to is the physical Turing test: a robot whose work in the physical world you cannot tell apart from a person's.

We are working towards a fully in-house stack: energy, semiconductors, hardware, software, and the robots built on them.

Every robot first has to know where it is. That is where we started, and it is the first step of the stack: xclean, our real-time SLAM. We publish our results, losses included: we measure on public benchmarks and on our own recordings, write our rules down before the runs (and disclose every later amendment), and publish every result.

xclean

Live stereo visual-inertial SLAM.

xclean turns a stereo camera into a camera pose for every frame, while the robot moves, with loop closure, relocalisation and map reuse. Its GPU code is written in CuPy: no C++ build step, no ROS, no learned networks in the SLAM pipeline, native on Windows 11; from version 0.2.0 it also installs on Linux and NVIDIA Jetson Orin and Thor.

Sensors

Stereo, plus the IMU when it checks out

It uses the IMU while a built-in consistency check accepts it, and otherwise runs as stereo visual odometry with loop closure. In the final board the IMU stayed in use to the end on 50 of 216 runs; per dataset.

Camera

Luxonis OAK-D-Lite

Built for a Luxonis OAK-D-Lite: our OAK routes were recorded on it and replayed in real time, and the release runs live on it (so far tested with the camera standing still on a tripod).

Full SLAM

Loops, relocalisation, map reuse

Image work on the GPU, a sliding-window factor graph on the CPU, and loop corrections applied live.

Some of these wins are against a baseline that failed in part: 3 of the 10 against ORB-SLAM3 (diverged runs or a partial trajectory) and 1 of the 11 against OKVIS2-X (a diverged run). Both baselines are visual-inertial systems, though ORB-SLAM3 ran stereo-only on four Rosario routes (its IMU start-up crashed). The OKVIS2-X comparison uses frame-level ATE, as pre-registered.
